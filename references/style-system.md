# Style System

## Visual thesis

The image should feel as though a landscape has been distilled into one quiet artifact and suspended in a luminous gallery-like void. It is not an empty background with a cutout pasted on top. It is an act of reduction: remove narrative clutter while preserving the source's most memorable visual evidence.

## Recognition hierarchy

Preserve, in order:

1. silhouette and orientation;
2. distinctive structural features;
3. key source color or light cue;
4. essential reflection or connected formation;
5. small internal textures that support recognition.

Sacrifice incidental scenery, minor objects, clutter, and literal atmospheric continuity before any item above.

## Subject archetypes

### Landmark with reflection

- Treat the landmark and its undisturbed reflection as one visual unit.
- Keep the landmark upright and structurally recognizable.
- Use a small vertical footprint, roughly 18-25% of canvas height.
- Place below the center line; a slight lateral offset often feels more poetic.
- Let the reflection soften into translucent resin or glass, but do not crop or detach it.

### Salt ridge, shoreline, dune, or terrain edge

- Select one uninterrupted section rather than the entire landscape.
- Form a low, quiet horizontal island occupying roughly 35-55% of width.
- Keep total height below about 20% of the canvas.
- Preserve ridge rhythm and color separation between mineral and water.
- Use pale salt crystal, mineral dust, translucent shallow water, or porcelain-like deposits.

### Cloud break or light band

- Retain one elongated cloud layer or atmospheric band.
- Preserve the location and hue of the internal light opening.
- Avoid a dramatic sky or full cloudscape; the band must float alone.
- Use smoke crystal, semi-opaque glass, or mineral vapor with a restrained amber core.

### Other subjects

- Prefer one element with a strong silhouette or unique color cue.
- If removing the environment makes it unreadable, preserve the smallest connected fragment needed for context.
- Never combine unrelated fragments merely to make the canvas busier.

## Negative space

Target 70-80% visually quiet area. This percentage is optical, not a strict pixel mask. The space should be alive through extremely subtle:

- warm ivory, pearl gray, mist gray, or pale cyan tonal drift;
- soft top-left or overhead museum illumination;
- a barely visible central glow or edge falloff;
- low-noise, seamless depth without recognizable architecture.

Avoid pure flat white, strong gradients, visible paper grain, wall-floor seams, hard vignettes, ornamental texture, and obvious stage construction.

## Material language

Use one main material family and, at most, one supporting material. Material must clarify the source rather than become the subject.

- Frosted glass: soft translucency, blurred internal depth, controlled highlights.
- Translucent resin: preserved color embedded in a quiet clear body.
- Salt crystal: fine granular mineral sparkle with no jewelry-like glamour.
- Porcelain deposit: matte pale body with delicate irregular edges.
- Smoke crystal: semi-opaque gray volume suitable for cloud layers.
- Mineral vapor: softened particulate edge with stable overall silhouette.

Avoid chrome, neon plastic, high-gloss luxury products, faceted gemstones, excessive iridescence, dramatic melting, or chaotic liquid forms.

## Light and shadow

- Light: large, diffused, soft museum light; typically from top-left or overhead.
- Contrast: low to medium-low, with enough local contrast to read the subject.
- Shadow: faint, wide, low-opacity, and slightly detached when suspension is desired.
- No pedestal, plinth, acrylic stand, tabletop, or visible floor line.

## Series consistency

Lock these across all images:

- background hue and temperature;
- illumination direction;
- contrast range;
- subject-to-canvas scale logic;
- shadow softness and distance;
- abstraction intensity;
- compatible material vocabulary.

Allow geometry-aware variation in placement. A vertical landmark and a horizontal ridge should not be forced into identical coordinates.

## Hard exclusions

Never introduce:

- torn paper, deckled edges, folded paper, ripped openings;
- collage, scrapbook, pasted layers, frames, borders, or mats;
- titles, captions, labels, numbers, logos, watermarks, icons, stamps;
- unrelated objects or decorative props;
- visible gallery walls, product pedestals, tabletops, or showroom architecture;
- oversize subjects that destroy the negative-space balance;
- generic replacement landmarks or altered source identity;
- multiple duplicate subjects, incorrect reflections, or artificial symmetry.
